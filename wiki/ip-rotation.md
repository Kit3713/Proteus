"Does Proteus rotate my IP?" The honest answer is "yes, the local one, as a side effect of MAC rotation — and no, not the public one. For that you want a VPN or Tor." This page expands.

For the mental model, read `proteus wiki concepts` first. For the threat-scope discussion, `proteus wiki threat-model`.

## What "IP rotation" means

The phrase covers four different things, and Proteus's relationship with each is different.

1. **Local DHCP IP (DHCPv4).** The IPv4 address your gateway hands you when you join a Wi-Fi or Ethernet network. Local to that LAN. Visible to anything on the segment.
2. **Public IP.** The IPv4 or IPv6 address your traffic appears to originate from on the public Internet. Set by your ISP, your carrier, your VPN exit, or your Tor exit. Visible to every server you talk to.
3. **IPv6 IID.** The lower 64 bits of your IPv6 address. Self-derived from the MAC plus a network-scoped key (under stable-privacy) or directly from the MAC (under EUI-64). Visible on the wire and to every IPv6 destination you reach.
4. **DHCPv6 DUID.** The persistent client identifier sent in DHCPv6 requests. Not strictly an "IP", but it lives in the same identity layer and people group it with IP rotation in their heads.

Proteus addresses 1, 3, and 4 as a **side effect** of MAC rotation. Proteus does **not** touch 2 — that's VPN/Tor territory and explicitly out of scope per `proteus wiki threat-model`.

## Local DHCP IP (DHCPv4)

Rotating a MAC forces a DHCP renegotiation. The mechanism:

1. Proteus writes the new MAC to the NetworkManager connection profile (`wifi.cloned-mac-address` or `ethernet.cloned-mac-address`).
2. NetworkManager re-associates the interface, which triggers DHCP DISCOVER with the new client identity.
3. The gateway sees a "new" device asking for a lease and hands out a fresh IP from the pool.
4. The old lease is left to expire on the server side.

Effect: your local IP rotates with your MAC. You don't ask for it; it falls out of the rotation flow. There is no separate `proteus ip rotate` command — see "Why we don't add" below.

To confirm a fresh IP was issued:

```sh
ip addr show wlan0 | grep 'inet '
sudo proteus rotate --iface wlan0
ip addr show wlan0 | grep 'inet '
# expect a different IPv4
```

## Caveat: gateways may hand back the same IP

Many DHCP servers cache the previous lease in their pool and will hand the same IP back if it's still free, even to a different MAC. This is implementation-dependent — `dnsmasq`, ISC `dhcpd`, and consumer router firmwares all behave differently. The MAC change is what matters for tracking; the IP is downstream.

If the IP is what you care about (rare — observers correlate by MAC at L2, not by your local IP), the only reliable forcing function on consumer gateways is to wait out the lease or force the gateway to drop you. Neither is something Proteus drives.

## What passive observers actually see

Two views of "your IP":

- **On the LAN.** APs, switches, captive-portal vendors, and anyone passively listening on the L2 segment see your MAC, your DHCP options, and your local IP. They correlate visits by MAC primarily — the local IP is ephemeral and pool-dependent. Proteus's MAC rotation defeats this correlation. Local IP rotation comes along for the ride but is not what's doing the work.
- **On the public Internet.** Every server you talk to sees the public IP your packets arrive from. If you're behind your home ISP without a VPN, that's a stable per-household identifier. If you're behind a carrier-grade NAT (CGNAT), it's a per-carrier-region identifier shared with thousands of other subscribers — less precise, but still tied to your account on the carrier side. If you're behind a VPN or Tor, it's the VPN exit or Tor exit, which is what those tools exist to provide.

Proteus changes the first view. Nothing Proteus does changes the second view. If your threat model includes the second view, layer in the right tool — see `proteus wiki threat-model` § "Composition with other tools".

## IPv6 IID (interface identifier)

Proteus configures **stable-privacy** IID generation (RFC 7217) and **temporary** addresses (RFC 8981) per managed interface. Mechanism in `proteus wiki ipv6`.

- Stable-privacy IID derives from MAC + per-network secret. When MAC rotates, the stable-privacy IID rotates too.
- Temporary IPv6 addresses are random per RFC 8981 and regenerated by the kernel on its own timer (hours by default). They flush on the down/up cycle that accompanies MAC rotation.
- Link-local addresses (`fe80::/10`) follow the same `addr_gen_mode`, so they rotate with the MAC under stable-privacy.

Cross-ref `proteus wiki ipv6` for the full picture, including the EUI-64 leak case and verification recipes.

## IPv6 prefix vs IID — only the IID rotates

A common point of confusion. An IPv6 address is a 64-bit prefix plus a 64-bit IID. The prefix is announced by your upstream router and is the same for every device on the link. Proteus rotates the IID; the prefix is not Proteus's to change.

- Same network, fresh MAC: same prefix, fresh IID. The full address changes; the network half doesn't.
- Different network: different prefix, different IID (because stable-privacy is per-network-keyed). The full address changes.

If your prefix is what you wanted to rotate, you're back at "public IP rotation" — that's the upstream's call, and the answer is the same as the IPv4 answer above. Use a VPN, use Tor, use a different network.

## DHCPv6 DUID

The DUID is the persistent client identifier sent in DHCPv6. By default on most distros, the DUID is written to disk at first boot and reused across MAC changes — defeating the rotation.

Proteus configures NetworkManager to use a **link-layer DUID** (`ipv6.dhcp-duid = "ll"`) derived from the current MAC, so the DUID rotates whenever the MAC does. Per-interface, not system-wide.

Cross-ref `proteus wiki dhcp` for the full key list and verification recipes.

## What Proteus does NOT do for IP

- **Public IP rotation.** Your public IP is set by your ISP, your cellular carrier, your VPN exit, or your Tor exit. Proteus runs on your laptop; it does not control the path your traffic takes once it leaves the local segment.
- **VPN management.** Out of scope. Proteus does not bring tunnels up, down, or rotate exits. Use your VPN's own client.
- **Tor integration.** Out of scope. Use Tor Browser, the `tor` daemon, or `torsocks`.
- **Per-application IP.** Different apps using different exit IPs is a routing/policy problem solved by `iproute2` rules, network namespaces, or SOCKS proxies. Not Proteus.
- **DHCP release/renew without MAC change.** A one-liner via `nmcli` — see the recipe below. No reason to wrap it.

## What to use for public IP rotation

- **Tor Browser** — gold standard for low-volume privacy-sensitive browsing. Public IP changes per circuit, roughly every 10 minutes for a given destination, and you also get browser-fingerprint resistance. Composes cleanly with Proteus: see `proteus wiki hostile-environments`.
- **Tor system-wide** (via `torsocks`, transparent-proxy setups, or Whonix) — same circuit-rotation property applied to all socksified traffic. More setup, more breakage potential.
- **Mullvad VPN** — fixed exit IPs but accepts cash by mail for unlinkable accounts. Single hop, lower latency than Tor, higher bandwidth.
- **Other commercial VPNs** — varies widely. Investigate logging policy, jurisdiction, and ownership before trusting any of them. "No-logs" claims are unverifiable without an audit.
- **Cellular tethering** — your phone's modem gets a different IP from a different carrier path. But your phone's IMSI/IMEI is now the identifier the carrier sees; you've moved the problem rather than solved it.
- **Different physical network** — joining a different Wi-Fi network gives you a different gateway and a different public IP. Free, simple, doesn't compose with anything but works for "I want to look like a different person right now."

Proteus does not pick one for you. It rotates your network-join identity and gets out of the way. The rest of your privacy stack is `proteus wiki threat-model` § "Composition with other tools".

## Recipe: combining Proteus with a VPN

Proteus owns L2-L4, VPN owns the public IP. Order of operations:

1. **Pre-trip prep.** Rotate MAC, set hostname (or pin a generic), confirm Bluetooth alias is generic and discoverable=off, run `proteus status` to confirm everything's `applied`.
2. **Join the network.** Proteus's known-portal handling kicks in if it's a captive-portal SSID; auth as needed.
3. **Bring up the VPN before sending real traffic.** Pre-VPN traffic still leaks a TLS ClientHello with your real IP visible to the local network. The VPN client itself does not, because that's the connection you're securing.
4. **Use Proteus's normal cadence.** Every 2h (default), Proteus rotates MAC, IID, DUID. Local IP changes with them. The VPN tunnel may briefly drop and reconnect; most VPN clients reconnect to a different exit on their own cadence.
5. **Pin the MAC if the VPN is bound to it.** Some corporate VPNs key authentication off the client MAC. `proteus pin --connection "Corporate VPN"` freezes the MAC for that profile while leaving everything else rotatable.

Combined effect: the local LAN sees a fresh MAC plus a fresh local IP each rotation. The public Internet sees the VPN's exit IP, which changes on the VPN's own cadence (or when you reconnect manually).

## Recipe: force-renew local IP without rotating MAC

Sometimes you want a fresh DHCP lease without the disruption of a MAC change — for example, troubleshooting a sticky lease or working around a captive-portal session you can't easily clear.

```sh
sudo nmcli connection down "MyWiFiSSID"
sudo nmcli connection up "MyWiFiSSID"
```

This triggers a DHCP DISCOVER with the **same** MAC. The gateway may give you the same IP (if the previous lease is still in its pool and the implementation prefers stickiness), or a different one (if the lease expired or the implementation rotates the pool). Behavior is up to the gateway.

If you want this every time, the reliable way is to rotate the MAC: `sudo proteus rotate --iface wlan0`. The new client identity guarantees the gateway treats it as a new device.

## Recipe: confirm what local IP you have right now

```sh
proteus current --json | jq '.interfaces[] | {name, mac, ipv4, ipv6}'
```

The same data is in `ip addr show <iface>`, but `proteus current` filters out the link-local noise and pairs the IP with the MAC and DUID for that interface, which is what you usually want when answering "did the rotation actually take?"

## Reverting and IP

`proteus revert --iface wlan0` restores the original cached MAC for that interface, which causes a DHCP renegotiation under the original identity. The local IP issued under that lease is whatever the gateway hands the original MAC at that moment — usually different from the IP you had during rotation, because the lease pool has moved on.

`proteus revert` does not "restore" any IP; IPs are not Proteus's to cache. The original MAC is the only restored thing. Everything downstream (IP, IID, DUID) re-derives from there.

## Why we don't add `proteus ip rotate` as a separate command

1. **Local IP rotation is downstream of MAC rotation.** A separate command would be redundant — the user already runs `proteus rotate`, and the IP changes as a side effect.
2. **Public IP rotation is out of scope.** A `proteus ip rotate` command that didn't change the public IP would surprise users; one that did would mean Proteus had grown a VPN client. Per `proteus wiki threat-model`, that's not what Proteus is.
3. **Force-renew without MAC change is already a one-liner.** `nmcli connection down/up` is two commands. Wrapping it would add API surface for no real gain.

A `proteus rotate --release-dhcp` flag, for the case where someone wants the renew to be explicit and atomic with the MAC change, is on the rough roadmap at low priority. Open an issue if you want it.
